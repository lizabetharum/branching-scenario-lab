Three practice conversations built to change what people do, not to get them to the last screen. Two pharmacy leadership scenarios run as open conversations: the character holds a packet of facts and reveals each one only when the learner’s questions earn it. A classroom scenario runs as a fixed branching tree for comparison. Every debrief scores observable behavior, and every design, privacy and guardrail decision is published in full.

The Label Conversation

A confident pharmacy manager coaches a technician after three labeling errors in two weeks. Telling or leading makes the technician more guarded. Two open questions surface the cause the error log can’t show: a label tray two technicians share.

  • GROW coaching, scored on open questions before any interpretation
  • A second case with a new technician, error pattern and cause
  • Hints on request, recorded apart from independent performance

The Pickup Counter

A first-time manager talks with a technician who has been rushing customers. The technician explains nothing until she names the concern, and a question she answers herself earns nothing. The cause only surfaces if she asks: one person covering the drive-through and pickup at once.

  • Built around a new manager’s failure modes: softening, self-answering, avoiding
  • Automatic support after moves that don’t help
  • Written reflection, reviewed by a person, not the AI

Can You Just Fix It?

A student’s micro:bit robot drives off the line after three changes, minutes before the qualifying run, while a second student waits for help. The tree follows Christy Tucker’s sequence: write the ideal path first, then play each mistake through to its consequence, with a way to recover.

  • A plausible wrong lead and a student who wants to revert everything
  • Trade-off costs listed in the debrief, apart from the scores

The Problem

Most branching scenarios measure completion. Will Thalheimer’s Learning-Transfer Evaluation Model (LTEM) treats attendance and completion as the weakest evidence of learning, below decision-making, task performance and transfer to real work. This project starts from Christy Tucker’s advice to name the observable behavior, the common mistakes and their consequences before writing any dialogue. Each scenario has an objective an observer could check. Each debrief separates how the story ended from what the learner did, so an agreed plan can still come with a missed objective.

Two Formats, One Standard

The classroom scenario is a fixed tree. Every node and consequence is authored, which makes it cheap to test and easy to compare across learners. The pharmacy scenarios have no tree. Each turn makes up to three model calls with separate jobs: one labels the learner’s behavior and which hidden topic a question is about, one plays the character using only facts already earned, and one checks the reply for leaks. Code applies fixed rules to the model’s labels to decide which facts are revealed, how guarded the character is, when the conversation ends and every score, so a wrong label means a wrong score. Every label appears in the debrief and can be corrected on a facilitator review page. (The NIST Generative AI Profile calls for context-specific evaluation, monitoring and human oversight. A prompt provides none of those on its own.)

The pharmacy scenarios contain no clinical content by design: no drugs, no doses, no patients. Clinical facts would need clinician approval before any learner saw them.

After the Debrief

Every debrief ends with three steps toward real work: a commitment in the learner’s own words, a calendar reminder to practice a new case a week later, and a one-page checklist a colleague can use in the learner’s next real conversation. An authoring kit lets a subject-matter expert build a new case from one template, then checks and playtests it before anyone else sees it.

How It Was Reviewed

A colleague reviewed the site twice against the research it is built on. The first review found that asking for a plan counted as agreeing to one, and the second found feedback that misstated what learners had uncovered. Both were fixed, and every score now has to quote something the learner actually said or confirmed. The full revision log is on the design page.

What This Does Not Show Yet

No learner has used it. Every scenario, rubric and fact packet is a design specification, not a validated intervention, and a self-review against the scenario-design rubric sets the release decision at “revise before learner use.” The next evidence to collect: think-aloud sessions with pharmacy leaders and teachers, a pharmacist’s review of both pharmacy cases, and human ratings of the AI’s behavior labels. The built-in facilitator review page is designed to collect that last one.
